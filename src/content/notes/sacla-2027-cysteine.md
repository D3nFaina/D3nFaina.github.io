**When and where:** February 2027, beamline BL3 of SACLA, the Japanese X-ray free-electron laser in Harima.

Hard-XFEL pulses near 7 keV do not just probe a liquid; they change it. Recent experiments showed that liquid water undergoes substantial ionization-driven structural and electronic changes within the first 100 fs of intense irradiation. Those studies looked at neat water and at X-ray observables. How the **optical** response of a chemically relevant aqueous biomolecular solution evolves on the same timescale is still largely unexplored.

That is what we will measure. The XFEL pulse at 7.0 keV is the pump, off resonance, and a synchronized UV pulse is the probe. We will record the pump-induced UV absorption change (ΔOD) in aqueous **cysteine**.

### Why UV absorption, and why cysteine

When ionizing radiation hits a liquid, it produces photoelectrons, Auger decay and cascades of secondary electrons. These start ultrafast radiolysis and modify the electronic structure of both the solvent and the solute.

UV transient absorption is a direct window on these processes. It is sensitive to solvated electrons, radicals and short-lived ionization-induced changes in the liquid environment.

Cysteine is a good model system because sulfur-containing biomolecules are known to be modified substantially by X-rays, through sulfur-centred reaction pathways and oxidation.

### The plan

- Measure ΔOD at a fixed delay of about 180 fs, the initial stage of the ionization-driven response.
- Scan the X-ray fluence, the cysteine concentration and the probe wavelength.
- Alternate pure water and cysteine solutions under identical conditions. This shows how a reactive solute changes the early response of the ionized water, and where radiation-induced spectral changes set in.

Order-of-magnitude estimates put the signals between a few 10⁻⁵ and the 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³ range, depending on fluence, focusing and stability. That is enough to map how ΔOD depends on absorbed dose.

The result should be two things:

- quantitative signal scales and stability limits for ultrafast UV spectroscopy under realistic liquid-jet XFEL conditions;
- a direct look at the earliest radiation-driven dynamics in aqueous biomolecular systems.

The experiment needs only standard SACLA capabilities: 7.0 keV SASE operation, a liquid jet and a synchronized UV probe. It needs no special operating modes or beamline modifications.
